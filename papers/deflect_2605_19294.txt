                                                  Abstract: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies increasingly rely on asyn-
                                                  chronous inference to hide large-model latency behind ongoing robot motion.
                                                  While this avoids the stop-and-go behavior of synchronous action-chunk exe-
                                                  cution, it creates a prediction–execution mismatch: the next chunk is computed
                                                  from a stale observation at inference start but executed only after the robot and
                                                  scene have evolved. As a result, actions that fit the prediction-time state can be-
                                                  come misaligned with the execution-time state. Existing runtime repair, behavior-
                                                  cloning, and preference-alignment approaches do not directly teach the policy
                                                  to resolve this stale-input mismatch. We propose DEFLECT, an offline post-
                                                  training framework for delay-robust asynchronous VLAs. DEFLECT converts
                                                  latency-induced mismatch into counterfactual preference supervision: a frozen
                                                  reference VLA generates a preferred chunk from the future execution-time ob-
                                                  servation and a rejected chunk from the stale prediction-time observation. The
                                                  trainable policy scores both chunks under the same deployment-time input, learn-
                                                  ing to favor execution-time-aligned actions while a supervised fine-tuning anchor
                                                  preserves the expert action manifold. DEFLECT requires no human preference
                                                  labels, reward models, online robot rollouts, architectural changes, or additional
                                                  inference-time computation. Across Kinetix, LIBERO, and three real-robot tasks,
                                                  DEFLECT improves delay robustness over strong asynchronous VLA baselines,
                                                  raising high-latency success by up to 6.4 percentage points and achieving a 4.6
                                                  percentage-point gain at the longest delay on a real-scale VLA.
1       Introduction

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies have shown strong performance across a wide range of
robot manipulation and locomotion tasks [1, 2, 3, 4]. Many of these policies are deployed syn-
chronously and predict actions as action chunks [5]: at each decision step the model outputs a short
sequence of actions, the robot executes them in order, and the policy only runs again to produce the
next chunk after the current one is finished. This execution scheme makes robot motion choppy:
after each chunk, the robot must wait for the next inference to complete, during which it cannot
respond to scene changes. To remove this pause, asynchronous inference has become a common
deployment strategy for real-time VLA execution [6, 7, 8], in which the robot keeps executing the
current chunk while the model computes the next one in parallel. Because each action chunk typi-
cally takes longer to execute than a single inference pass, the next chunk can be computed before the
current one finishes, effectively hiding inference latency behind ongoing robot motion. This overlap
also allows the policy to issue new chunks more frequently than stop-and-go execution, improving
responsiveness to scene changes.

However, asynchronous inference also creates a prediction–execution mismatch: the next chunk is
computed from an earlier observation but executed after the robot and scene have changed. The
resulting action chunk may therefore no longer fit the state the robot actually faces when execution
begins. As latency grows, this mismatch accumulates over continuous action execution: each step
may look reasonable on its own, but the robot drifts from the correct trajectory and eventually fails
the task. Figure 2 shows the severity of this problem: at prediction time, the vial lies at the center
of the conveyor, and the predicted action chunk targets pellet release at that position. By the time
the chunk is executed, the vial has moved to the other end of the conveyor, causing the pellets to
miss the target. Existing asynchronous strategies [6, 8, 9, 10] can soften this mismatch, but they
do not address the central issue: the action chunk should match the execution-time world, not the
prediction-time view.
To address this execution-time alignment gap, we propose DEFLECT1 , an offline post-training
framework that teaches asynchronous VLAs to prefer actions that remain correct when executed.
DEFLECT turns prediction–execution mismatch into counterfactual preference supervision: a

2     Background and Motivation
2.1   Asynchronous VLA Deployment

We consider an action-chunking VLA policy that outputs a chunk of actions at each inference call.
Inference takes d control steps, then a chunk predicted from the observation at time t is executed only
after the robot and scene have evolved for d steps. This creates a mismatch between the prediction-
time input and the execution-time state.

2.3   Our Insight

DEFLECT’s key insight, illustrated in Figure 3, is to use the unattainable zero-latency branch as
an offline preference oracle for the deployable async branch. The key difference is the observa-
tion timestamp: zero-latency assumes access to the latest execution-time observation (ot+d , st+d ),


                                                          3
whereas the async policy must act from the stale prediction-time view. DEFLECT uses this contrast
to define a temporal counterfactual preference: the chunk aligned with the execution-time world is
preferred over the chunk generated from the stale view. It then learns this preference under the same
stale deployment input used at test time, shifting the policy toward actions that remain correct when
executed without changing the runtime interface.

This construction is counterfactual. The deployed policy will not observe (st+d , ot+d ) when pre-

When the scene changes little over d steps, A+   t and At may be nearly identical, producing little
preference signal. When the scene or robot state changes substantially, the pair becomes high-
contrast and provides the supervision needed for delay robustness. Thus, this construction naturally
adapts the strength of the preference signal to the severity of the prediction–execution mismatch.

4     Experiments
We evaluate DEFLECT on two simulated benchmarks, Kinetix [17] and LIBERO [18], and three
real-robot tasks on a bimanual arm setup. Unless noted, Kinetix results average 12 tasks × 1024
rollouts per cell, LIBERO results average 500 episodes per suite per delay, and simulation baselines
use the released VLASH [6] checkpoint. Real-robot experiments compare π0.5 [4], VLASH, and
DEFLECT under the same hardware protocol. Implementation details are in Appendices B–D; full
Kinetix/LIBERO tables in Appendices E–F; delay generalization, ablations, and the PFM baseline
in Appendices G–I; real-robot results, mechanism analyses, and training cost in Appendices J–N.

Delay realism. Our headline regime d ∈ {5, 6, 7} corresponds to ≈ 167–233 ms of hidden in-
ference time at 30 Hz, matching the inference cycles reported for current flow VLAs (π0.5 [4], X-
VLA [19]) on consumer GPUs even with state-of-the-art acceleration [20].

4.1   Simulation

Kinetix. Kinetix [17] comprises 12 contact-rich and locomotion tasks that demand fast reaction.
We train on delays d ∈ {0, . . . , 4} and evaluate on d ∈ {0, . . . , 7}, so d ∈ {5, 6, 7} is the unseen-
delay generalization regime. Each predicted chunk contains 8 actions, which the robot executes
sequentially. Meanwhile, the model takes d control steps to compute the next chunk. By the time
it is ready, the robot has used up d actions, leaving only 8 − d from the current chunk to bridge the
transition. At d ∈ {5, 6, 7}, this overlap shrinks to just 1–3 actions, which is too short for runtime
schedulers like RTC and BID that rely on the overlap to splice the new chunk in smoothly.
As shown in Figure 5 and Table 1, the naive rollover, RTC, and BID all collapse to ≤ 5% success
in this regime. VLASH remains functional but degrades from 74.6 at d=5 to 60.5 at d=7. Our
DEFLECT enables asynchronous control to remain effective at higher inference delays, averaging
73.5 over d ∈ {5, 6, 7} compared to VLASH’s 67.1, a substantial improvement of +6.4 on average
and up to +7.0 at d=6.
To confirm that these high-delay gains come from DEFLECT itself rather than a cosine-restart
artifact, and that they generalize beyond the training delay range, we run two additional experiments

LIBERO. LIBERO [18] is an image-based manipulation benchmark with four task suites. We use
it to test whether the Kinetix gains transfer to a π0.5 backbone, the same VLA architecture deployed
in our real-robot study. On the 4-suite average, DEFLECT matches or improves over VLASH at
every delay, with the improvement growing from +0.2 at d=1 to +4.6 at d=7. Aggregating over the
4 suites (N =2,000 episodes per method per delay), the d=7 gain is statistically significant under the
95% Wilson CI of the difference (+4.6 ± 2.7 pp; Appendix F).2 Per-suite results are in Appendix F.

4.2               Real-World Evaluation

We test DEFLECT on a real bimanual arm setup with three tasks that stress different aspects of
delay-robust execution. Conveyor-I is a two-step pick-and-place: the left arm places a cup on a
moving conveyor, and the right arm must retrieve it before it leaves the workspace. Conveyor-II
adds a middle step in which the right arm must drop the red cube (not the green one) into the
cup before retrieval. Whack-a-Mole is a single-arm reactive task in which moles surface briefly at
random positions and the arm must strike each one before it retracts. We report full-task success rate
on the conveyor tasks, requiring all sub-goals to be achieved, and mean moles struck per 30-second
trial on Whack-a-Mole. Each cell uses N =30 trials, with per-sub-goal breakdowns in Appendix J.
On Conveyor-I, π0.5 achieves 76.7%, VLASH reaches 86.7%, and DEFLECT reaches 96.7%,
improving over VLASH by +10 pp and over π0.5 by +20 pp. On Conveyor-II, which adds a middle
sub-goal that tightens the time budget, π0.5 collapses to 46.7%, VLASH recovers to 83.3%, and
DEFLECT further reaches 90.0%. On the reactive Whack-a-Mole, π0.5 , VLASH, and DEFLECT
strike 8.9, 10.4, and 13.6 moles per 30-second trial respectively.

5   Conclusion

We presented DEFLECT, a fully offline refinement that converts inference delay into counterfac-
tual preference supervision for flow-matching VLAs. The learned correction is dynamics-aware
and variance-preserving rather than mode-collapsing, extending the usable delay envelope of asyn-
chronous VLAs into the regime where runtime-scheduling baselines collapse, all without changing
inference, adding a scheduler, or requiring human or reward labels. Across Kinetix, LIBERO, and
three real-robot tasks, DEFLECT improves high-delay execution, achieving a 6.4 percentage-point
gain over VLASH on Kinetix, a 4.6 percentage-point gain on LIBERO, and consistent improvements
over both π0.5 and VLASH on real robot.


6   Limitations

DEFLECT’s fresh/stale preference signal is weakest at low delays, where the two observations are
nearly identical and the labeled contrast is small, so the DPO contribution mainly appears in the
high-delay regime. We also instantiate FM-DPO for flow-matching action heads and have not yet
tested whether the same surrogate extends to autoregressive heads with different likelihood forms.
More broadly, DEFLECT suggests a general counterfactual-supervision recipe: whenever an ideal-
but-undeployable condition and a deployable condition can be simulated offline, both candidates
can be scored under the deployment context to provide a zero-cost preference signal, e.g., for full-
precision versus quantized action heads, full-sensor versus sensor-dropout perception, or teacher
versus distilled policies.

A    Related Work

VLA policies and delay-robust execution. Modern VLA policies span several architectural fami-
lies: autoregressive discrete-token decoders (RT-2 [1], OpenVLA [2]); cross-embodiment generalist
transformers (Octo [21], GR00T N1 [22]); continuous-action heads based on CVAE (ACT [5]) or
diffusion (Diffusion Policy [23], RDT-1B [24]); and flow-matching policies (π0 [3], π0.5 [4]). The
last family is the class we target. Inference latency in such policies has been tackled along two
complementary axes. Runtime-side methods operate on a frozen base policy at deployment: RTC
inpaints the residual chunk prefix at inference boundaries [8], BID performs closed-loop action
resampling under forward-contrast and backward-coherence criteria [9], and A2C2 applies a correc-
tion head at test time [10]. Training-time interventions instead modify the supervised pipeline itself:
VLASH rolls the proprioceptive state forward at training to align prediction with the execution-
time state [6], and AsyncVLA combines an asynchronous flow-matching objective with a learned
confidence rater that triggers partial chunk regeneration [7]. Both lines share a common limita-
tion: runtime methods do not change the policy itself, and training-time methods stay within be-
havior cloning. Under a stale observation, neither teaches the policy to favor the action that fits the
execution-time scene over the action that only fits the stale view. DEFLECT addresses this gap by
refining the policy parameters through preference optimization. It leaves inference unchanged and
is therefore composable with any runtime scheduler.

Preference optimization for continuous policies. Preference optimization originated in deep RL
from human preferences [25] and matured into RLHF [26] and DPO [15, 27], with subsequent
variants such as IPO [28]. The paradigm has been extended to diffusion image generation [14],
VLA refinement with human labels [11], and flow matching itself via Preference Flow Matching
(PFM) [13], which trains an add-on flow module from expert-vs-policy preferences on clean obser-
vations. We differ in two ways: (i) we target fully offline flow-matching VLA post-training rather
than online RL, and (ii) our preference labels are generated automatically from latency-induced
fresh/stale counterfactuals rather than from human labels, reward models, or clean-observation ex-
pert comparisons. In our experiments (Table 1), a clean-conditioned preference signal applied under
stale observations does not improve on the base policy at d ≥ 2, suggesting this distinction matters
in practice.

B    Implementation Details

Preference-pair generation. Both A+ and A− are produced online from the frozen VLASH
reference policy, not from the dataset, so the pair always lives on the deployable action manifold
(Algorithm 1). The preferred action A+ is sampled from the reference policy conditioned on the
future observation (st+d , ot+d ). The rejected action A− is sampled with identical Gaussian noise
and ODE schedule, but conditioned on the earlier observation (st , ot ). We use dmax =4 on both
benchmarks. The async-training context delay dctx is sampled from {0, 1, 2, 3, 4} per example,
while the DPO-pair delay dDPO is drawn from {1, 2, 3, 4}. d=0 is skipped from dDPO because A+
and A− would coincide, degenerating the pair, while dctx =0 is kept so the SFT anchor still sees the
synchronous context. Evaluation extends to the larger test-time range d ∈ {0, . . . , 7} without any
retraining. For LIBERO the DPO data budget is 200 steps × batch 8 = 1,600 pair-uses per suite,
sampled with replacement from a small pre-collected pool produced by 8 parallel environments
running the frozen reference policy. For Kinetix the reference policy is fast enough to sample pairs
on-the-fly, so no offline pool is materialized.

Training. We use AdamW with a cosine schedule, a 1,000-step linear warmup, and decay to
1% of peak LR. Shared hyperparameters across both benchmarks are β=1.0 for the DPO loss,
λSFT =1.0 for the SFT anchor, and context delays dctx sampled uniformly from {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}
with DPO-pair delays dDPO drawn from {1, 2, 3, 4}. Benchmark-specific differences are in Ta-
ble 2. The DPO weight λDPO is tuned per benchmark. On Kinetix we sweep λDPO ∈
{0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5} and select 0.02 (see Figure 11). On LIBERO we use λDPO =0.1.

resamples chunks under forward-contrast and backward-coherence criteria. Both rely on the resid-
ual chunk prefix from the previous prediction, which shrinks to 1–3 steps at d ≥ 5 and becomes too
short to support either method. Other concurrent VLA work includes architectural variants (Hybrid-

inference-acceleration methods (DeeR-VLA [35], SnapFlow [36], MP1 [37]), and runtime guidance
modules (DynaGuide [38]). These target architectural design, inference cost, or runtime steering
rather than delay-robust execution and are orthogonal to our refinement.

G    Zero-Shot Delay Generalization

A natural concern is that our DPO preference pairs are sampled from a limited training-delay pool
(dDPO ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}, Section 3.1), yet we evaluate at delays as high as d=7. To test whether DE-
FLECT transfers the correction mechanism beyond its training distribution, we train two additional
variants that differ from the main run only in the DPO-pair delay sampler: Model A (narrow)
with dDPO ∈ {1, 2}, Model B (main run, dDPO ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}), and Model C (covers test) with
dDPO ∈ {1, . . . , 7} (Model C additionally extends the asynchronous interval to 8). Model A, trained
with DPO pairs only at d ∈ {1, 2}, still recovers +3.7 at d=7 over VLASH (a delay more than 3×
beyond its DPO training range) and captures roughly 70% of the full gain of the test-range-covering
Model C. The bulk of the robustness improvement therefore transfers zero-shot to unseen delays.

I       Preference-Signal Baseline: Preference Flow Matching (PFM)

Preference Flow Matching [13] was originally proposed for general flow-based generative-policy
alignment and is not a VLA method. For a fair comparison we re-implement it within our VLA
training pipeline. Conceptually, PFM trains an add-on flow module on top of a frozen reference
policy using (expert, reference) preference pairs drawn on clean observations, then corrects the refer-
ence’s chunk at test time by integrating its own flow conditioned on the test-time (stale) observation.
Our re-implementation is matched to DEFLECT in every respect (same F LOW P OLICY architecture,
same VLASH initialization, same Kinetix dataset, same 24-epoch cosine schedule, same compute),
so the only controlled differences are (i) the preference-pair construction (expert-vs-policy on clean
o for PFM vs. fresh-vs-stale counterfactual for DEFLECT) and (ii) the training loss (PFM uses its
own flow-matching regression loss, while DEFLECT uses the FM-DPO loss in Eq. (3)). PFM’s
loss exposes a single hyperparameter σ that controls how smoothly the flow regresses to the target
action. We evaluate two settings of this knob: σ=0 (the strictest, no smoothing) and σ=0.1 (the
value used in the PFM paper). Reporting both rules out the possibility that any gap to DEFLECT
is just a tuning artifact of σ.

K    Mechanism: What DEFLECT Changes in the Flow-Matching Policy

preservation is in fact strengthened by the selection: even at maximally-divergent states, DEFLECT
does not collapse the action distribution. Figure 13 shows per-task correction magnitude, and Fig-

L       Restart-Effect Decomposition (VLASH vs VLASH- RETRAINED vs
        DEFLECT)

Any method that resumes training from a pretrained checkpoint inherits a non-trivial lift from new
optimizer state and LR warmup. We call this the restart/cosine effect. To give reviewers a transpar-
ent decomposition of DEFLECT’s delay-averaged +3.9 lift over VLASH, we train an additional
dedicated control VLASH- RETRAINED: the same VLASH checkpoint, the same 24-epoch cosine
schedule, the same optimizer and batch size as DEFLECT, but with λDPO =0 (i.e., pure SFT con-
tinuation with no preference term).


Interpretation. Of the +3.9 delay-averaged improvement of DEFLECT over VLASH reported
in Table 3, roughly +2.6 is attributable to the cosine restart alone (any continued-training run on top
of VLASH would inherit it), and the remaining +1.3 is the net contribution of preference alignment.
Crucially, ∆DPO is essentially zero at d ∈ {0, 1, 2} and climbs monotonically to +2.3 at d=6,
demonstrating that preference alignment concentrates its benefit precisely in the delay regime where
fresh and stale observations produce different actions (the high-contrast regime for our preference
pairs). A pure-SFT schedule change cannot reproduce this delay-dependent structure, since nothing
in the λ=0 objective is aware of the fresh/stale distinction. The clean isolation is further corroborated
by the LIBERO results: LIBERO fine-tunes run for only 200 gradient steps, so the cosine-restart
artifact is negligible, yet the d=7 gain there reaches +4.6, i.e., the preference objective by itself
carries most of the signal when the restart confound is absent.

N    Training-Time Cost of DEFLECT

DEFLECT does not change inference at all, since the flow-matching ODE is solved exactly as for
the VLASH base policy. It does add an offline training cost relative to simply continuing SFT, with
different breakdowns on Kinetix and LIBERO due to where the preference pairs are sourced.

Kinetix (on-the-fly pair sampling). For each gradient step the reference policy is queried twice to
draw A+ and A− by running its flow-matching ODE for 5 denoising steps each, contributing 2×5 =
10 reference forward passes. The trainable-velocity evaluation at the shared x±
                                                                              τ adds one trainable
forward and one backward, and the reference-velocity evaluation needed by the full DPO margin in
Eq. (6) adds one more reference forward. Per gradient step this totals 11 reference forwards plus
1 trainable forward+backward, compared with 1 trainable forward+backward for vanilla SFT. All
reference passes are wrapped in stop gradient and do not contribute to the gradient computation.

LIBERO (offline pre-collected pool). Preference pairs are not sampled on-the-fly; instead a one-
time offline collection pass runs the reference policy over the demonstration suite. Under our default
setting (4 suites × 4 delays × 10 tasks × 10 inits × ∼ 230 steps per episode × 10 flow steps), this
amounts to roughly 3.7 × 106 reference forwards as a fixed up-front cost. Once the pool is built,
each gradient step costs 2 trainable forward+backward (SFT and DPO) plus 1 reference forward for
the full-margin term, i.e. about 3× the per-step compute of vanilla SFT.

Implications. The reference forwards dominate the wall-clock overhead on Kinetix because they
happen inside every gradient step; on LIBERO they are amortized to a one-time collection cost. In
both cases the additional compute is entirely offline. Inference is unchanged, and the trained policy
is queried with the same cdep
                           t  and the same flow-step budget as the original VLASH backbone, so
DEFLECT remains a drop-in replacement for deployed VLASH checkpoints.
